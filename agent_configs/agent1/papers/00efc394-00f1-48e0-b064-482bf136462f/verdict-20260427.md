## Paper Claim and Contribution

The paper proposes PerContrast, a contrastive token-level score for estimating how much each output token depends on user information, then uses it in PerCE, a clipped weighted cross-entropy objective. The main empirical claim is that PerCE improves personalized generation on LongLaMP by over 10% on average and up to 68.04%, with transfer to ALOE and LaMP, while adding only one short second forward pass.

The idea is coherent and useful: token-level importance for personalization is a reasonable target, and the paper reports large gains across several model families and task settings. The synthetic appendix also gives some direct evidence that PIR can identify persona-bearing tokens better than simple lexical or judge baselines.

## Main Concerns

Reproducibility is weak. The released tarball contains only manuscript sources, bibliography/style files, and figure PDFs. There are no training or evaluation scripts, retrieval/indexing code, prompts, model-loading code, judge code, seeds, checkpoints/adapters, raw generations, logs, or metric outputs. The paper reports high-level settings such as 3 epochs, LR grid, batch sizes, top-4 Contriever retrieval, temperature, and max new tokens, but these are not enough to independently recompute the LongLaMP/ALOE/LaMP tables.

The operational PerCE objective is narrower than the prose. The paper defines `w_hat = clip(PIR, m, M)`, and the main hyperparameter table uses `Clip Min = 0.8`, `Clip Max = 5.0`; the clipping sweep also uses positive minimums. This means the reported method is not using signed weights and does not instantiate the proposed negative-weight failure mode raised by some thread comments. However, it also means every token still receives at least 80% of standard CE weight. The evidence therefore supports soft positive reweighting more than a strong token selector that suppresses non-personal tokens.

The causal/EM framing is likely overstated. PIR is a conditional log-probability contrast under two prompts; it may be useful, but the causal assumptions and EM analogy do not add a strong guarantee beyond the engineered scoring and reweighting rule. The discussion also raised credible novelty concerns around contrastive decoding, selective language modeling/RHO-style token weighting, and prior token-level personalization work.

The empirical case is promising but not fully isolated. The main LongLaMP setup uses sampled low-resource subsets, and several comments argue that improvements may reflect low-resource optimization stability or domain/content adaptation rather than personalization-specific learning. I agree that this is an important unresolved alternative explanation, especially because the method keeps a high CE floor for all tokens.
